    Guide

    How it works

    You write the chords. The engine decides where every voice goes and how it travels there, and you can reshape each part of that without losing the rest.

    1Find your way around the top bar

    The bar across the top holds everything about the piece as a whole. From left to right:

    Library. Your saved pieces and the examples. You can also open a piece file here.
    Name
    Piece name and save dot. Click the name to rename the piece. The dot is hollow if the piece has never been saved, green when everything is saved, and amber when there are unsaved changes.
    New, Save, Save as new. New writes a fresh piece from a new harmony seed. Save as new keeps the original and saves a copy.
    Undo and Redo. Step back and forward through your changes, including rerolls.
    HarmonyVoicing
    Layer locks. A piece has four layers, and each one gets its own seed: the number its random choices start from. A locked layer (filled in, with a closed padlock) stays exactly as it is when you reroll. An unlocked layer (outlined, with an open padlock) gets a new seed. Click a lock to switch it.
    Reroll. Gives every unlocked layer a new seed, all at once. It's greyed out when everything is locked.
    Settings and Guide. Trace looks, themes and accent color; and this Guide.

    What each layer covers when it's rerolled:

    • Harmony: the chords. Rerolling it writes a whole new progression in place of yours, so it starts locked.
    • Voicing: which note each voice sings, within the same chords. How far a reroll can wander is set by Variation on the Voicing tab.
    • Motion: the order voices leave in, when the departure order is set to random.
    • Sound: each voice's detune and place in the stereo field, and the shape of the reverb.

    For example: keep Harmony locked, unlock Voicing, and press Reroll. You hear the same chords sung by a different arrangement of voices. Don't like it? Reroll again, or Undo.

    2Write the harmony

    On the Harmony tab, type chord names, one per line. A few extras: =G#6 gathers every voice onto one note, Amaj9:6 holds a chord for 6 seconds, and Emaj9[E2 B3 G#4 …] pins an exact voicing. The harmony seed can write a whole progression for you. Keep it locked to protect chords you've written.

    3Shape the voicing

    The Voicing tab decides which note each voice sings. Choose how many voices, then balance the sliders: continuity (small steps), coverage (every chord note present), stacking (unisons avoided or welcomed), spacing and variation. Each slider is a weight in the score that picks every voice's next note, as step 9 of The math explains. The highest note and the bass rule set the range.

    4Set the motion

    The Motion tab controls how voices travel: the glide time, the shape of the curve, and the departure order. Add stagger and the voices leave one after another, low to high, high to low, largest leap first, or at random.

    5Choose the sound and the look

    The Sound tab picks the tone and sets detune, stereo width, reverb and brightness. In Settings, a trace look decides how the lines are drawn (phosphor, laser, aurora and more), and the interface theme and accent color dress the page.

    6Lock what you love, reroll the rest

    Four seeds (harmony, voicing, motion and sound) make every random choice repeatable. The lock buttons in the top bar (step 1) keep a layer as it is, and Reroll gives every unlocked layer a fresh seed. Keep the chords, reroll the voicing, and listen. Undo brings back anything you liked better.

    7Save, and reopen exactly

    The Library keeps your pieces in this browser. A piece file stores the chords, the seeds, every setting and the resolved notes. If a future version voices chords differently, an older piece still plays its saved notes, until you change its chords, voicing or motion, or choose Re-voice with this engine.

    8Export

    The Export tab saves a piece file, WAV and MP3 audio, MIDI with every glide kept as pitch bend, chord-only MIDI, a MusicXML score, or everything at once. Video plays the piece once and records it, using the piece's trace look.

    9Works offline

    Chord Glide is a single page. Open it from a file on your computer or from a website, and every export downloads straight to your computer. Keep lame.min.js beside it for MP3 export; nothing else is needed, not even an internet connection.

    The math, step by step

    You don't need any music theory or algebra to follow this. Each step builds on the one before, and the small tools let you try things as you go.

    1A note is something vibrating

    Every sound is air being pushed back and forth. How many times it does that each second is its frequency, measured in hertz (Hz). The A that orchestras tune to vibrates 440 times a second, so it's 440 Hz. More vibrations per second sound higher.

    2Doubling makes an octave

    Double a frequency and you hear the same note, only higher. 220 Hz, 440 Hz and 880 Hz are all the note A, each one an octave above the last.

    Your ears hear ratios, not differences. Going from 220 to 440 Hz sounds like the same size jump as 440 to 880 Hz, even though the second jump is twice as many hertz. Keep that in mind; it explains everything that follows.

    3Twelve equal steps fill an octave

    Western music splits every octave into 12 steps called semitones. On a piano that's every key, white and black, from one C up to the next C.

    For the 12 steps to sound equal, each one has to multiply the frequency by the same amount. Which number, multiplied by itself 12 times, gives 2? About 1.0595. So each semitone raises the frequency by roughly 6%.

    4Every key gets a number

    To do math with notes, each piano key gets a number. This tool uses the standard MIDI numbering: middle C is 60, the key just above it (C♯) is 61, and the tuning A is 69. One number up is one semitone up, and 12 numbers up is an octave.

    5The formula that turns numbers into sound

    f(n) = 440 × 2(n − 69) ÷ 12

    Read it from the inside out:

    • n − 69: how many semitones the note is above the tuning A (negative means below).
    • ÷ 12: how many octaves that is. Three semitones is a quarter of an octave.
    • 2…: double once for every octave. Two to the power of a quarter is about 1.189.
    • 440 ×: start from the tuning A and scale it.

    Example: the C above the tuning A is note 72. Then 72 − 69 = 3, and 3 ÷ 12 = 0.25, and 20.25 ≈ 1.189, and 440 × 1.189 ≈ 523.25 Hz.

    6Cents: the pitches in between

    Between two neighboring notes there are 100 cents. While a voice glides, it passes through every pitch in between. That's why labels show things like D5 +43¢: 43 hundredths of the way from D5 up toward D♯5. The formula still works for those; n can be 74.43.

    7A chord is a set of note names

    A chord is several notes sounding together, and its name tells you which ones. The letter is the root. The rest adds notes counted up from it: maj means major (bright), m means minor (darker), and numbers like 7, 9, 11 and 13 add extra notes on top. Which octave a note sits in doesn't change its name; any E counts as an E.

    8Many voices, few note names

    Emaj9 has five note names, but a piece might have 11 or 14 voices. Each voice picks one of those names and an octave to sing it in, so some names show up in several octaves: E2, E3 and E5, for example. Spreading the voices out from low to high like this is called a voicing.

    9Moving with the least effort

    When the chord changes, every voice needs a new note. The engine looks for the arrangement where the voices move as little as possible in total, counting semitones. Musicians call this smooth voice leading.

    To choose, the engine gives every possible arrangement a score and keeps the lowest. The score adds up:

    • Movement: the semitones each voice travels, with big leaps costing extra.
    • Missing notes: a penalty if one of the chord's notes isn't sung by anyone.
    • Unisons: a penalty when two voices land on exactly the same note.
    • Spacing: a small preference for voices spread evenly rather than bunched up.

    Those are the sliders on the Voicing tab; each one changes how much its rule counts. With 14 voices there are billions of possible arrangements, far too many to try one by one. So the engine works upward from the lowest voice, keeping only the cheapest way to reach each possibility. It's like finding the shortest route on a map without driving every road. (The technique is called dynamic programming.)

    10The bass follows its own rule

    The lowest voice usually sings the chord's root, like the E of Emaj9, or the note after the slash in a chord like Amaj7/B. That gives the harmony a floor to stand on. The Bass setting offers a few other rules.

    11Gliding on an S-curve

    Voices don't jump between notes. Each one slides along a curve that starts slowly, speeds up in the middle, and settles gently. The default curve is called smoothstep:

    s = 3x² − 2x³

    Here x runs from 0 (the start of the glide) to 1 (the end), and s says how far along the voice is. The dashed line shows a plain straight slide for comparison.

    The slide happens in note numbers, not hertz. Halfway through a glide from note 60 to note 64, the voice is at note 62, and then the formula from step 5 turns that into hertz. Because your ears hear ratios (step 2), this sounds even. Sliding in hertz instead would sound lopsided: rushed at the bottom, slow at the top. Over an octave from C, the halfway point in hertz is already a G, seven of the twelve semitones up.

    12Seeds: randomness you can repeat

    A few choices are left to chance: how widely the first chord spreads out, which of two equally good arrangements wins, and the order voices leave in. A seed is the starting number for the random choices. The same seed always gives the same "random" results, so a piece can be rebuilt exactly. The four seeds (harmony, voicing, motion and sound) let you reroll one layer and keep the rest.

    13All together

    1. You write chord names.
    2. Each chord becomes a set of note names.
    3. The voicing gives every voice one note in one octave, moving as little as possible.
    4. Each voice glides to its new note along the S-curve, in note numbers.
    5. The formula turns every in-between note number into hertz.
    6. An oscillator per voice vibrates at those frequencies, and you hear the chord change.
    7. The same numbers draw the lines you see.

    Engine history

    The engine turns your chords, seeds and settings into notes and timing. Its version only changes when the same piece could come out differently.

    The promise is simple: the same chords, seeds and settings on the same engine give the same notes, in any browser on any computer. Every saved piece records the notes it was voiced with and which engine voiced them. A piece from a different engine plays exactly as saved until you change its chords, voicing or motion; then the current engine voices it again.

    The current engine is 3.1, unchanged since app 3.1. Later releases touched the engine's code without changing a single voicing (saving trace looks in pieces, stricter checks on saved notes, the new name), so the number stayed the same.

    3.1Hard limits and saved notesapp 3.1

    • Highest note is a hard ceiling. Upper voices are searched from the bass + 3 semitones up to exactly the Highest note setting. Engine 3.0 stretched the range to at least an octave above that floor, so a low setting could be exceeded. The opening chord can no longer pass it either: when its usual range (a fifth above the bass up to the setting − 4) has no chord tones, it now widens only down toward the bass, where engine 3.0 widened it an octave both ways.
    • Tight registers. If no chord tone fits between the bass + 3 and the ceiling, the floor drops to the bass + 1. If nothing fits even then, the upper voices hold the bass note and the chord is flagged.
    • Nearest chord tone only considers the chord's own pitch classes, preferring the root on a tie. Engine 3.0 also counted a slash-chord bass note, so the bass could leave the chord.
    • Saved notes (realizations). Each saved piece stores every chord's target notes, plus each voice's departure offset and glide duration, tagged with the engine version. On opening, notes from the same engine are discarded, because the settings rebuild them exactly. Notes from a different engine play as saved.
    • File values are clamped to the controls' ranges: chord length 1.5–12 s, glide 0–5 s, tempo 30–180, Highest note G4–C7. Files with a newer format are refused instead of being silently changed.

    3.0Controls and separate seedsapp 3

    • Four seeds. Harmony, voicing, motion and sound each have their own seed. Every chord draws from its own random stream, mulberry32(seed ⊕ (i + 1) × 0x9E3779B1 ⊕ salt), where i is the chord's position and the salt differs for voicing and motion. A chord's random choices depend only on its seed and position, so rerolling one layer never disturbs another.
    • The voicing controls set the cost weights of the voice-leading search (The math explains the search):

      move = continuity ÷ 50   miss = 3 × coverage ÷ 50
      dup = 3 × (1 − stacking ÷ 50)   gap = 0.01 × spacing ÷ 25
      jitter = max(0.004, 3 × variation ÷ 100)

      At the defaults these equal engine 2's fixed weights, so with Variation at 0 engine 3.0 voices pieces as engine 2 did, apart from exact ties. Stacking above 50 makes dup negative, which rewards voices sharing a note.
    • Bass modes. Root or slash note, nearest chord tone, pedal (the first bass held throughout), or none (every voice joins the search). The bass sits in a chosen octave, 1 to 3.
    • Register. Highest note replaced the fixed ceiling of E6, and the opening spread reaches the setting − 4 (engine 2 used C6).
    • Motion. Each voice now has its own departure time and glide length. With stagger s (0 to 1), each moving voice glides for g × (1 − 0.75s), and their departures are spread evenly across the remaining g × 0.75s, ordered low to high, high to low, largest leap first, smallest first, or randomly from the motion seed. Voices that don't move aren't staggered.
    • Piece files (format 3) record every setting needed to rebuild a piece.

    2Reliabilityapp 2

    • Chords with more than 8 notes. Each search state tracks which chord tones are covered in 12 bits instead of 8, so large chords no longer overflow into the pitch part of the state.
    • Shuffles are the same in every browser. Seeded shuffles use Fisher–Yates instead of sorting with a random comparison, whose result depends on the browser's sort algorithm.
    • Slash chords. The bass note is no longer added to the chord's tones, so in C/D the upper voices no longer pick up a D.
    • A second try, then a warning. If no voicing fits within 6 semitones of every voice, the search retries within 12. If that fails too, each voice takes its nearest chord tone and the chord is flagged.
    • The top voice moves like the others. Engine 1 made it 10% more reluctant to move.
    • More spellings: ø7, Maj and MAJ.

    1First engineapp 1

    • Glides in semitones. Pitch moves in semitone space and sounds at f(n) = 440 × 2(n − 69) ÷ 12, eased by smoothstep, smootherstep, cosine or linear.
    • Bass on its note between C2 and B2.
    • Voice leading. Upper voices are assigned together by dynamic programming, from the bottom voice up, never crossing. Each considers chord tones within 6 semitones. A move of d semitones costs |d| + 0.1d²; landing on the same note as the voice below costs 1.5; each gap g between neighbors costs 0.01g²; each chord tone left out costs 3.0; and a seeded jitter of up to 0.004 breaks exact ties.
    • Opening chords. Out of a single note, chord tones are spread from a fifth above the bass up to C6, and the voices glide there over max(2.4 s, 1.9 × glide).

    File format

    Piece files are JSON, marked "format": "chord-glide-piece" with "formatVersion": 3 since app 3. Fields added since are optional, so older versions simply ignore them: saved notes (realization, app 3.1) and trace looks (view.trace, app 3.3). Top-level fields this version doesn't know are kept when a piece is saved again.

    About

    Chord Glide is a songwriting instrument by Noizgenie. Write chords, hear them as gliding voices, and shape how they move.

    Use it online at chord-glide.noizgenie.com, or get the source code and the downloadable app on GitHub.

    Privacy

    Chord Glide itself has no accounts, ads, analytics or tracking, and sets no cookies. The voicing, the sound, the video and every export are made in your browser, and Chord Glide never uploads what you write or play. Exported files go straight to your computer.
