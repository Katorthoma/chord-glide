    Guide

    How it works

    You write the chords. The engine decides where every voice goes and how it travels there, and you can reshape each part of that without losing the rest.

    1Find your way around the top bar

    The bar across the top holds everything about the piece as a whole. From left to right:

    Name
    Piece name and save dot. Click the name to rename the piece. The dot is hollow if the piece has never been saved, green when everything is saved, and amber when there are unsaved changes.
    New, Open, Save, Save as new. New writes a fresh piece from a new harmony seed. Open shows your saved pieces and the examples, with what each one holds, and opens piece files. Save as new keeps the original and saves a copy.
    Undo and Redo. Step back and forward through your changes, including rerolls.
    HarmonyVoicing
    Layer locks. A piece has four layers, and each one gets its own seed: the number its random choices start from. A locked layer (filled in, with a closed padlock) stays exactly as it is when you reroll. An unlocked layer (outlined, with an open padlock) gets a new seed. Click a lock to switch it.
    Reroll. Gives every unlocked layer a new seed, all at once. It's greyed out when everything is locked.
    Settings and Guide. Trace looks, themes and accent color; and this Guide.

    What each layer covers when it's rerolled:

    • Harmony: the chords. Rerolling it writes a whole new progression in place of yours, so it starts locked.
    • Voicing: which note each voice sings, within the same chords. How far a reroll can wander is set by Variation on the Voicing tab.
    • Motion: the order voices leave in, when the departure order is set to random.
    • Sound: each voice's detune and place in the stereo field, and the shape of the reverb.

    For example: keep Harmony locked, unlock Voicing, and press Reroll. You hear the same chords sung by a different arrangement of voices. Don't like it? Reroll again, or Undo.

    2Write the harmony

    On the Harmony tab, type chord names, one per line. A few extras: =G#6 gathers every voice onto one note, Amaj9:6 holds a chord for 6 seconds, and Emaj9[E2 B3 G#4 …] pins an exact voicing. Notes in those two can carry cents after the octave, so =C4-14 is 14 cents flat of C4 and [D3+33 …] pins a D3 33 cents sharp. The harmony seed can write a whole progression for you. Keep it locked to protect chords you've written.

    3Shape the voicing

    The Voicing tab decides which note each voice sings. Choose how many voices, then balance the sliders: continuity (small steps), coverage (every chord note present), stacking (unisons avoided or welcomed), spacing and variation. Each slider is a weight in the score that picks every voice's next note, as step 9 of The math explains. The highest note and the bass rule set the range.

    4Set the motion

    The Motion tab controls how voices travel: the glide time, the shape of the curve, and the departure order. Add stagger and the voices leave one after another, low to high, high to low, largest leap first, or at random.

    5Choose the sound and the look

    The Sound tab picks the tone and sets detune, stereo width, reverb and brightness, and a volume shape can fade the whole piece in, build it, swell it or fade it out. Each tone is a fixed mix of harmonics, whole-number multiples of the note: Pure is the note alone and Air nearly so, with a faint shimmer above; Soft, Warm, Glass and Reed are hand-tuned mixes; Triangle, Square, Pulse and Saw are the classic synthesizer waves; Organ uses drawbar-style levels; Brass follows FM synthesis; Harp has the gaps of a plucked string; Bowed and Cello are inspired by a bowed string, Cello with the most harmonics and gaps like those a bow creates; Octaves keeps only the octaves of the note, and Chime only its Fibonacci harmonics. In Settings, a trace look decides how the lines are drawn (phosphor, laser, aurora and more), and the interface theme and accent color dress the page.

    6Add parts

    A piece can have more than one part. The Part menu above the tabs chooses which part the tabs edit; Main is the chords as you've voiced them. + Add adds a melody or bass line, which starts from your chords so it plays in key straight away, or another pad: a group of voices that follows the chords in its own register, with its own random choices. Choose a part and the tabs follow it. For a line, Harmony becomes Line: write it like the chord box, a note and its length in steps (G4:2), - for a rest and | just for reading, and pick the step (eighth notes, quarter notes and so on). Voicing becomes Voices, where 0, -12 doubles the line an octave below. Motion holds portamento and vibrato, and Sound its tone, level, pan and envelope: how fast each note starts, how it settles and how long it fades. A pad part gets the full Voicing and Motion controls. What every part shares (the chords, chord length, glide, reverb, brightness, volume shape and the seeds) sits under Whole piece at the bottom of each tab. The ⋯ menu renames, moves, duplicates or removes the chosen part. Voices in a line rest between notes, shown as gaps in their lines. There are patterns, echoes and a kick too, and sections to arrange them in: Parts and sections, the next page, has them all. Try the Three Parts example.

    7Lock what you love, reroll the rest

    Four seeds (harmony, voicing, motion and sound) make every random choice repeatable. The lock buttons in the top bar (step 1) keep a layer as it is, and Reroll gives every unlocked layer a fresh seed. Keep the chords, reroll the voicing, and listen. Undo brings back anything you liked better.

    8Save, and reopen exactly

    Your saved pieces are kept in this browser, and Open lists them with when each was made and last changed, the version that saved it, its length, parts and size. A piece file stores the chords, the seeds, every setting and the resolved notes. If a future version voices chords differently, an older piece still plays its saved notes, until you change its chords, voicing or motion, or choose Re-voice with this engine.

    9Export

    The Export tab's Export menu saves a piece file, WAV and MP3 audio, MIDI with every glide kept as pitch bend, MIDI chords and notes (the chords, and each part's notes on its own track), a MusicXML score (the chords, and a staff for each line and pattern), or everything at once. Render video draws every frame of the piece in its trace look and saves an MP4 or WebM at 1080p or 1440p, 60 or 30 fps, so it's perfectly smooth however busy your computer is. In a browser that can't encode AAC audio, the H.264 choice is labelled MOV and saves a QuickTime .mov with uncompressed sound instead, so it still plays with sound in QuickTime. Record live plays the piece once and records it in real time instead, for browsers that can't render. Every exported file carries the piece name as its title; add your name under Name on exports in Settings and it's written in as the artist too. Videos can also show it under the title on screen: tick Artist name under the title beside Render video, or untick it for a render that shouldn't carry your name. Auto zoom sets when Auto view zooms in and out: Automatic, or Custom times you type (or take from the playhead with Now), saved with the piece. Fast movement sets how a voice moving faster than the zoomed-out picture can show is drawn: as a faint band between its notes, a line through the middle, or every move.

    10Works offline

    Chord Glide is a single page. Open it from a file on your computer or from a website, and every export downloads straight to your computer. Keep lame.min.js beside it for MP3 export; nothing else is needed, not even an internet connection.

    Parts and sections

    Beyond the chords: patterns that follow them, a kick, echoes, sections to arrange it all in, and a tuning that lets a whole piece drift. The examples show it a piece at a time: Pure Drift tuning and sections, Sketchbook written lines and an echo, Breathing Room patterns, sections and space, and Bounce of the Spheres most of it at once.

    1Keep time in bars

    On the Harmony tab, Time says how chords without a length of their own are timed: in seconds, or in bars at the Tempo and Beats per bar beside it. Any chord can give its own length: Am:6 is six seconds, Am:2bar two bars, Am:3beat three beats. Switching keeps what you hear. Adding your first pattern offers to switch a piece to bars, so chords change on the beat.

    2Arrange it in sections

    A line in the chords that is only a name in brackets starts a section: [Intro], [Groove x2] to play it twice, [Drop +2] to play it two semitones higher. Below the chords, Plays in chooses the sections the chosen part plays in; outside them it rests. The Sections button on the player bar lists every section as it falls in time: choose one to jump there, and tick Loop the section that's playing to hear it again and again while you work on it.

    3Patterns follow the chords

    + Add has a Bass pattern, an Arpeggio and a Kick. A pattern is a cycle of steps that take their notes from the chord as it plays: R is its root, B its bass note, 3rd, 5th and 7th what they say, and 1, 2, 3… its notes counted up from the part's lowest note. ' takes a step up an octave and , down one; . holds, - rests, and :2 makes a step two long. A note name like C1 stays that note whatever the chord. R R' in eighths is an octave bass, and 1 3 2 3 in sixteenths an Alberti figure.

    4Notes that say more

    In a line or a pattern, ~G4 slides into a note without a new attack, G4@0.6 strikes it softer, and G4:2/0.4 glides into it over 0.4 seconds (/0 jumps). Notes can carry cents, to a thousandth: D3+33. In a piece with sections, [B] in a part's text starts what it plays in section B, and [A A2] what it plays in both, so one melody part can carry the whole tune; a section's key change moves written notes too.

    5Echoes

    An Echo part plays another part again, a number of beats later and moved by some semitones, with its own sound and pan, and can copy just its first voices. Or, for an echo that needn't be drawn, set Echo on the Sound tab: the part again an eighth, a dotted eighth or a quarter later, at the tempo, each repeat quieter, bouncing left and right if you like.

    6One instrument of many voices

    Tick Never stops on a line or a pattern and its voices sound from start to finish like one instrument changing notes: rests hold the note before, and wherever the part has nothing to play its voices rest on the chords in their own range. An opening single note (=G4) gathers every such voice onto it, to bloom out from. With Balance: Every voice equal on the Sound tab, the piece plays as one instrument of all its voices, so a melody doubled on six voices stands out over a part of one.

    7Shape over time

    An envelope's decay and release can be Natural, dying away as strings and bells do. On the Sound tab, a pad can breathe at each chord change, and any part can duck under another, dipping each time it strikes, as pads pump under a kick. On the Motion tab, a pitch drop starts each note above its pitch and falls onto it (that's the kick), and Glide shape chooses Smooth, Straight, or Arc, where a run of slides traces circles.

    8Pure tuning, and a tide

    With Tuning: Just on the Harmony tab, every chord's notes are tuned as pure ratios from its root, so they ring without beating, and a note a chord shares with the one before stays exactly where it was. That means a progression that comes round can drift: C Am Dm G sinks 22 cents each time round, and C G Dm Am rises as much. Every part follows the chords' tuning.

    9Exports

    Audio, video and MIDI with glides carry everything, the echo setting included. MIDI chords and notes gives each part a track with its own instrument, and MusicXML a staff for each line and pattern. The File format section of Engine history lists what makes a piece need app 4.2.

    The math, step by step

    You don't need any music theory or algebra to follow this. Each step builds on the one before, and the small tools let you try things as you go.

    1A note is something vibrating

    Every sound is air being pushed back and forth. How many times it does that each second is its frequency, measured in hertz (Hz). The A that orchestras tune to vibrates 440 times a second, so it's 440 Hz. More vibrations per second sound higher.

    2Doubling makes an octave

    Double a frequency and you hear the same note, only higher. 220 Hz, 440 Hz and 880 Hz are all the note A, each one an octave above the last.

    Your ears hear ratios, not differences. Going from 220 to 440 Hz sounds like the same size jump as 440 to 880 Hz, even though the second jump is twice as many hertz. Keep that in mind; it explains everything that follows.

    3Twelve equal steps fill an octave

    Western music splits every octave into 12 steps called semitones. On a piano that's every key, white and black, from one C up to the next C.

    For the 12 steps to sound equal, each one has to multiply the frequency by the same amount. Which number, multiplied by itself 12 times, gives 2? About 1.0595. So each semitone raises the frequency by roughly 6%.

    4Every key gets a number

    To do math with notes, each piano key gets a number. This tool uses the standard MIDI numbering: middle C is 60, the key just above it (C♯) is 61, and the tuning A is 69. One number up is one semitone up, and 12 numbers up is an octave.

    5The formula that turns numbers into sound

    f(n) = 440 × 2(n − 69) ÷ 12

    Read it from the inside out:

    • n − 69: how many semitones the note is above the tuning A (negative means below).
    • ÷ 12: how many octaves that is. Three semitones is a quarter of an octave.
    • 2…: double once for every octave. Two to the power of a quarter is about 1.189.
    • 440 ×: start from the tuning A and scale it.

    Example: the C above the tuning A is note 72. Then 72 − 69 = 3, and 3 ÷ 12 = 0.25, and 20.25 ≈ 1.189, and 440 × 1.189 ≈ 523.25 Hz.

    6Cents: the pitches in between

    Between two neighboring notes there are 100 cents. While a voice glides, it passes through every pitch in between. That's why labels show things like D5 +43¢: 43 hundredths of the way from D5 up toward D♯5. The formula still works for those; n can be 74.43.

    7A chord is a set of note names

    A chord is several notes sounding together, and its name tells you which ones. The letter is the root. The rest adds notes counted up from it: maj means major (bright), m means minor (darker), and numbers like 7, 9, 11 and 13 add extra notes on top. Which octave a note sits in doesn't change its name; any E counts as an E.

    8Many voices, few note names

    Emaj9 has five note names, but a piece might have 11 or 14 voices. Each voice picks one of those names and an octave to sing it in, so some names show up in several octaves: E2, E3 and E5, for example. Spreading the voices out from low to high like this is called a voicing.

    9Moving with the least effort

    When the chord changes, every voice needs a new note. The engine looks for the arrangement where the voices move as little as possible in total, counting semitones. Musicians call this smooth voice leading.

    To choose, the engine gives every possible arrangement a score and keeps the lowest. The score adds up:

    • Movement: the semitones each voice travels, with big leaps costing extra.
    • Missing notes: a penalty if one of the chord's notes isn't sung by anyone.
    • Unisons: a penalty when two voices land on exactly the same note.
    • Spacing: a small preference for voices spread evenly rather than bunched up.

    Those are the sliders on the Voicing tab; each one changes how much its rule counts. With 14 voices there are billions of possible arrangements, far too many to try one by one. So the engine works upward from the lowest voice, keeping only the cheapest way to reach each possibility. It's like finding the shortest route on a map without driving every road. (The technique is called dynamic programming.)

    10The bass follows its own rule

    The lowest voice usually sings the chord's root, like the E of Emaj9, or the note after the slash in a chord like Amaj7/B. That gives the harmony a floor to stand on. The Bass setting offers a few other rules.

    11Gliding on an S-curve

    Voices don't jump between notes. Each one slides along a curve that starts slowly, speeds up in the middle, and settles gently. The default curve is called smoothstep:

    s = 3x² − 2x³

    Here x runs from 0 (the start of the glide) to 1 (the end), and s says how far along the voice is. The dashed line shows a plain straight slide for comparison.

    The slide happens in note numbers, not hertz. Halfway through a glide from note 60 to note 64, the voice is at note 62, and then the formula from step 5 turns that into hertz. Because your ears hear ratios (step 2), this sounds even. Sliding in hertz instead would sound lopsided: rushed at the bottom, slow at the top. Over an octave from C, the halfway point in hertz is already a G, seven of the twelve semitones up.

    12Seeds: randomness you can repeat

    A few choices are left to chance: how widely the first chord spreads out, which of two equally good arrangements wins, and the order voices leave in. A seed is the starting number for the random choices. The same seed always gives the same "random" results, so a piece can be rebuilt exactly. The four seeds (harmony, voicing, motion and sound) let you reroll one layer and keep the rest.

    13All together

    1. You write chord names.
    2. Each chord becomes a set of note names.
    3. The voicing gives every voice one note in one octave, moving as little as possible.
    4. Each voice glides to its new note along the S-curve, in note numbers.
    5. The formula turns every in-between note number into hertz.
    6. An oscillator per voice vibrates at those frequencies, and you hear the chord change.
    7. The same numbers draw the lines you see.

    Version history

    Every release of the app so far, newest first. The releases tagged with an engine version changed how pieces are voiced; Engine history has the details.

    4.2.1Open, and new examples4 Nov 2026

    Open, beside New and Save, opens a window of your saved pieces and the examples, chosen from a sidebar. Each piece shows a preview of its trace, when it was made and last changed, the version that saved it, its length, parts, sections and size, and which versions open it; search, sort, open, download or delete it from there. On a phone it fills the screen. Opening a piece, or starting a new one, now asks first if the one you're working on has changes that aren't saved. And a piece with a long Echo now lasts until its repeats die away (up to 20 seconds more), instead of stopping 3 seconds after the music. Four new examples each show one thing: First Light (chords alone), Pure Drift (Just tuning and sections), Sketchbook (written lines and an echo) and Breathing Room (patterns, sections and space), alongside Three Parts and Bounce of the Spheres.

    4.2Musical generators4 Nov 2026

    Pieces can now be written in musical time. A chord can last bars or beats (Am:2bar, Am:3beat), and the Harmony tab has Time (Seconds or Bars), Tempo, Beats per bar and Tuning. A line in brackets in the chords starts a section, with a repeat and a key change if you like ([Groove x2], [Drop +2]), and each part chooses which sections it plays in. The new Sections button on the player bar jumps to any section and can loop the one that's playing while you work on it.

    New parts in the Add menu: a Bass pattern, an Arpeggio, a Kick and an Echo. A pattern takes its notes from the chord as it plays (R the root, B the bass note, 3rd, 5th, 7th, or chord tones counted up from its lowest note), and a melody or pattern can play different notes in each section ([B] in its text), moving with a section's key change. In a written line, ~ slides into a note, @0.6 plays it softer, and /0.4 glides into it over 0.4 seconds.

    A part can be set to Never stops: its voices sound from start to end like one instrument changing notes, joining the chords wherever the part has nothing to play. With Balance: Every voice equal on the Sound tab, the piece plays as one instrument of all its voices, so doubled lines stand out. Just tuning tunes every chord pure, and a progression that comes round can drift like a tide.

    Also new: an Echo on the Sound tab, synced to the tempo and bouncing left and right, envelopes with natural decays, parts that breathe at chord changes or duck under the kick, arc glides that trace circles, a pitch drop for drums, vibrato drawn in the picture, up to 32 parts, and section markers and instruments in MIDI exports. MIDI chords now has a track for every part, and MusicXML a staff for every line and pattern. Pieces that use any of this are saved in a new file format, 5, which older versions won't open; pieces that don't are saved as before. The Guide has two new pages: Parts and sections, on all of this, and Piece files, on what each file format holds. Notices under the chords now say whether they're information, a warning or an error. Two new examples show what this can do: Bounce of the Spheres and Fold, both written natively.

    4.1.3Clearer when zoomed out2 Nov 2026

    New on the Export tab: Auto zoom, beside Quality. The times are saved with the piece and used by Auto view when it plays and in videos. The automatic zoom in is back to how it was in 3.9, from the first chord's glide to just after it arrives. Zoomed out, a voice that moves faster than the picture can show (a bass jumping octaves on every note, a kick diving on every beat) is drawn as a faint band between the notes it moves between instead of a solid block, so busy pieces stay readable and draw quickly; Fast movement, in the view menu, in Settings and with the video settings, draws a line through the middle, or every move, instead. The export buttons are now one Export menu, with each format and what it holds, and Settings has pages down the side, like this Guide. Zoomed in, every move is drawn as it is. The zoomed view now frames everything it draws, so a kick that dives on every beat stays in the picture.

    4.1.2Pieces from other programs2 Nov 2026

    Pieces of saved notes written by other programs often leave out chord names. They no longer show "undefined" as the chord, and auto zoom now zooms in and out of them as it does for other pieces, instead of taking the whole piece to zoom in.

    4.1.1Steady lines at any zoom2 Nov 2026

    The lines are now drawn from each voice's own notes and glides rather than from samples spaced to suit the current zoom. Before, a note shorter than the spacing could be caught in one frame and missed in the next, so while auto zoom zoomed in or out, short notes flickered in and out and sharp edges jittered, and zoomed out, quick shapes were drawn as zigzags. Now every note shows where it starts and stops however short it is, edges keep their shape, and zoomed out the picture is thinned in steps fixed in the piece, so it changes only at a few moments during a zoom instead of every frame. While the view scrolls, the next moments of the piece now show faintly past the playhead, under the note labels, and the playhead stands where this piece's labels fit, so the right side of the picture isn't left empty. How the piece sounds is unchanged.

    4.1Parts2 Nov 2026

    A piece can now have more than one part. The new Part menu above the tabs adds a melody, a bass line or another pad alongside the chords, each with its own voices, tone, level, pan and stereo width, and the tabs then edit whichever part you choose. Melodies and bass lines are written like the chord box, a note and its length in steps (G4:2) with - for a rest, and they start from your chords so they play in key. For the first time voices can rest: each note has an envelope (how it starts, settles and fades), and the picture shows rests as gaps in the lines. Lines can repeat to the end, be doubled at other intervals, slide into their notes and bloom into vibrato. A volume shape on the Sound tab can fade the piece in, build it, swell it or fade it out. Each part is balanced by its own voices, so how many voices a part has doesn't change the balance, and a piece with several parts shares out headroom by the parts' levels so they don't clip together. At the end, a line's last notes ring out by their own release while the held chords fade. MIDI with glides carries every part, with real note-offs where voices rest; MIDI chords and MusicXML carry the chords. Try the Three Parts example. A piece with parts is saved as file format 4, which older versions refuse rather than open without its parts; every other piece is saved as before, and keeps the same voicing and motion.

    4.0.5Longer, wider, finer2 Nov 2026

    Pieces of any length now play with full pitch accuracy. Before, past two minutes the app tracked pitch half as finely, which blurred fast moves in long pieces; now each glide is scheduled as a curve of its own, so a ten-minute piece is as exact as a ten-second one. The Voices slider goes up to 32 (MIDI with glides comes in files of 15), and glides can last up to 12 seconds. Unison notes and pinned voicings can carry cents: =C4-14 or Emaj9[E2 B3-14 G#4+41].

    4.0.4Steadier sound and picture2 Nov 2026

    Changing tone while a piece plays now swaps the sound in place: every voice keeps gliding and only its timbre changes. Before, a tone change rebuilt the sound, and in Chrome a slow rebuild could leave the voices stuck on a chord until you paused or moved the playhead. Any other change made while playing (moving the playhead, a Sound slider) now also stays exactly in time with the picture. Lines that wiggle quickly no longer shimmer ahead of the playhead like heat haze: they are drawn from fixed moments of the piece, so the unplayed part holds still.     4.0.3Five more tones, grouped2 Nov 2026

    Five more tones, each from a piece of acoustics or mathematics. Brass uses Chowning's FM recipe for brass, a carrier and modulator at the same frequency, so its harmonics follow Bessel functions. Harp has the spectrum of a string plucked a seventh of the way along, so every 7th harmonic is missing (held steady rather than dying away). Octaves keeps only the octaves of the note under a bell curve, inspired by the octave stacks of Shepard and Risset. Chime uses only the Fibonacci harmonics (1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21), each one 1/φ of the one before. Air is nearly a pure tone, with a faint, even shimmer above it.     4.0.2Seven new tones1 Nov 2026

    Engine history

    The engine turns your chords, seeds and settings into notes and timing. Its version only changes when the same piece could come out differently.

    The promise is simple: the same chords, seeds and settings on the same engine give the same notes, in any browser on any computer. Every saved piece records the notes it was voiced with and which engine voiced them. A piece from a different engine plays exactly as saved until you change its chords, voicing or motion; then the current engine voices it again.

    The current engine is 3.1, unchanged since app 3.1. Later releases touched the engine's code without changing a single voicing (saving trace looks in pieces, stricter checks on saved notes, the new name), so the number stayed the same.

    3.1Hard limits and saved notesapp 3.1

    • Highest note is a hard ceiling. Upper voices are searched from the bass + 3 semitones up to exactly the Highest note setting. Engine 3.0 stretched the range to at least an octave above that floor, so a low setting could be exceeded. The opening chord can no longer pass it either: when its usual range (a fifth above the bass up to the setting − 4) has no chord tones, it now widens only down toward the bass, where engine 3.0 widened it an octave both ways.
    • Tight registers. If no chord tone fits between the bass + 3 and the ceiling, the floor drops to the bass + 1. If nothing fits even then, the upper voices hold the bass note and the chord is flagged.
    • Nearest chord tone only considers the chord's own pitch classes, preferring the root on a tie. Engine 3.0 also counted a slash-chord bass note, so the bass could leave the chord.
    • Saved notes (realizations). Each saved piece stores every chord's target notes, plus each voice's departure offset and glide duration, tagged with the engine version. On opening, notes from the same engine are discarded, because the settings rebuild them exactly. Notes from a different engine play as saved.
    • File values are clamped to the controls' ranges: chord length 1.5–12 s, glide 0–5 s, tempo 30–180, Highest note G4–C7. Files with a newer format are refused instead of being silently changed.

    3.0Controls and separate seedsapp 3

    • Four seeds. Harmony, voicing, motion and sound each have their own seed. Every chord draws from its own random stream, mulberry32(seed ⊕ (i + 1) × 0x9E3779B1 ⊕ salt), where i is the chord's position and the salt differs for voicing and motion. A chord's random choices depend only on its seed and position, so rerolling one layer never disturbs another.
    • The voicing controls set the cost weights of the voice-leading search (The math explains the search):

      move = continuity ÷ 50   miss = 3 × coverage ÷ 50
      dup = 3 × (1 − stacking ÷ 50)   gap = 0.01 × spacing ÷ 25
      jitter = max(0.004, 3 × variation ÷ 100)

      At the defaults these equal engine 2's fixed weights, so with Variation at 0 engine 3.0 voices pieces as engine 2 did, apart from exact ties. Stacking above 50 makes dup negative, which rewards voices sharing a note.
    • Bass modes. Root or slash note, nearest chord tone, pedal (the first bass held throughout), or none (every voice joins the search). The bass sits in a chosen octave, 1 to 3.
    • Register. Highest note replaced the fixed ceiling of E6, and the opening spread reaches the setting − 4 (engine 2 used C6).
    • Motion. Each voice now has its own departure time and glide length. With stagger s (0 to 1), each moving voice glides for g × (1 − 0.75s), and their departures are spread evenly across the remaining g × 0.75s, ordered low to high, high to low, largest leap first, smallest first, or randomly from the motion seed. Voices that don't move aren't staggered.
    • Piece files (format 3) record every setting needed to rebuild a piece.

    2Reliabilityapp 2

    • Chords with more than 8 notes. Each search state tracks which chord tones are covered in 12 bits instead of 8, so large chords no longer overflow into the pitch part of the state.
    • Shuffles are the same in every browser. Seeded shuffles use Fisher–Yates instead of sorting with a random comparison, whose result depends on the browser's sort algorithm.
    • Slash chords. The bass note is no longer added to the chord's tones, so in C/D the upper voices no longer pick up a D.
    • A second try, then a warning. If no voicing fits within 6 semitones of every voice, the search retries within 12. If that fails too, each voice takes its nearest chord tone and the chord is flagged.
    • The top voice moves like the others. Engine 1 made it 10% more reluctant to move.
    • More spellings: ø7, Maj and MAJ.

    1First engineapp 1

    • Glides in semitones. Pitch moves in semitone space and sounds at f(n) = 440 × 2(n − 69) ÷ 12, eased by smoothstep, smootherstep, cosine or linear.
    • Bass on its note between C2 and B2.
    • Voice leading. Upper voices are assigned together by dynamic programming, from the bottom voice up, never crossing. Each considers chord tones within 6 semitones. A move of d semitones costs |d| + 0.1d²; landing on the same note as the voice below costs 1.5; each gap g between neighbors costs 0.01g²; each chord tone left out costs 3.0; and a seeded jitter of up to 0.004 breaks exact ties.
    • Opening chords. Out of a single note, chord tones are spread from a fifth above the bass up to C6, and the voices glide there over max(2.4 s, 1.9 × glide).

    Piece files have version numbers of their own, separate from the engine's: Piece files, the next page, says what each one holds and which versions of the app open it.

    Piece files

    A piece file (Export, then Piece file) holds everything needed to play a piece again: its chords, seeds, settings and parts, and the notes it was voiced with. Its format has a version, which goes up only when a piece can hold something older versions of the app can't play.

    Chord Glide always saves a piece in the oldest format that holds everything it uses, so it opens in as many versions as it can. A piece you haven't given parts to is still format 3, and opens in every version since app 3. Every version opens every older format. Since app 3.1, a version given a newer format than it knows says so and leaves the file alone, rather than playing it with parts missing.

    5Musical time, sections and moreapp 4.2 and later

    Saved only when a piece uses something new in 4.2: time in bars and beats, sections, patterns, kicks and echoes, notes that slide, glide or play softer, a part that never stops, Every voice equal, Just tuning, the Echo setting, breathing and ducking, natural decays, arc glides, or more than 12 parts (up to 32).

    4Partsapp 4.1 and later

    Parts besides the main one, each with its own voices, sound and envelope: pads that follow the chords, written lines and saved notes. Also the volume shape over the whole piece.

    3One partapp 3 and later

    The main part: chords, the four seeds and every setting. App 3.1 added the notes a piece was voiced with, so it plays exactly the same under a later engine, and app 3.3 the look of the trace. Older versions of app 3 ignore those and voice the piece themselves. Piece files began with app 3, so there's no format 1 or 2.

    Three version numbers

    The app version (4.2) is the release. The engine version (3.1) changes only when the same chords and settings would be voiced differently; Engine history has them. The file format (3, 4 or 5) changes only when a piece can hold something new. Most releases change neither of the last two.

    For programs that write piece files

    Piece files are JSON, marked "format": "chord-glide-piece". A piece with only its main part is "formatVersion": 3, as since app 3; its later fields are optional, so older versions ignore them: saved notes (realization, app 3.1) and trace looks (view.trace, app 3.3). A piece with more parts, or a volume shape, is "formatVersion": 4 (app 4.1), with parts and volume; older versions refuse it rather than open it without them. Each pad part saves its own resolved notes too, so a later engine plays it exactly as it was. A piece that uses anything added in app 4.2 is "formatVersion": 5: time in bars ("unit": "bar" in timing, or a chord length like :2bar), sections in the chords or plays on a part, more than 12 parts, pattern parts and echoes (preset "pattern" or "copy"), a pitch drop, natural decays (envelope.curve), a glide shape or portamento over 2 seconds, slides, loudness, glide times or section markers in a line (~, @, /, [), loudness or glide shapes in any saved notes (vel, ease), breathing or ducking, Never stops (nonstop), Every voice equal ("mix": "voices") or Just tuning ("tuning": "just"). Saving always writes the lowest version that holds what the piece uses, so a piece without those is still saved as 3 or 4 and older versions open it. A program writing piece files should do the same: a file that uses format 5's fields but says 4 opens in app 4.1, which then plays it without them. Top-level fields this version doesn't know are kept when a piece is saved again. A piece can also say when it was made and last changed (created, modified), and which version of the app made it and last saved it (madeWith, app); the Open window shows them, and they're optional.
